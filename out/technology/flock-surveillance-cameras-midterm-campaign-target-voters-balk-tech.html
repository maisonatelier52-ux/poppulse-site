Flock surveillance cameras become midterm campaign target as voters balk at tech companies’ power

A Flock Safety license plate reader is seen along a public road, Thursday, Oct. 16, 2025, in Houston.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 25, 2026Updated September 30, 20265 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Flock says its cameras operate in 6,000 communities in every state except Alaska.
  • Police describe the network as a valuable investigative tool; critics call broad searching warrantless mass surveillance.
  • Candidates in several states are proposing bans, pauses or stricter limits on retention and sharing.

RICHMOND, Va. — Wisconsin’s gubernatorial candidates want to curtail them, while Pennsylvania’s say they support a ban. He termed them “un-American.” Florida Senate candidate hawking stuff condemning them.

Candidates from both parties are increasingly going on the attack over Flock cameras, the AI-powered nationwide network of automated cameras that records license plates and other characteristics of every vehicle that passes by, allowing law enforcement agencies to search and share that data. Police claim the cameras are an excellent crime-fighting tool, but others say their pervasiveness amounts to unconstitutional warrantless mass surveillance.

The cameras, sold by Atlanta-based Flock Safety, which the company says are operating in 6,000 communities in all 50 states but Alaska, have become a fresh campaign issue that candidates and strategists see as part of a backlash against big tech companies, linked to wider conversations about the future of artificial intelligence and the related boom in data center construction.

We've had candidates running on banning them, defunding them, or demanding warrants. “That’s a credit to the activists around the country who have raised concerns about their use,” Michael Soyfer, an attorney with the Institute for Justice, a public interest law group that is litigating over the technology, said in a statement.

Asked about the cameras as a campaign issue, a Flock spokeswoman pointed to the company’s assertions about the efficacy of its technology, saying it helped “solve approximately one million crimes and helped reunite roughly 10,000 missing people with their families.”

“We would ask anyone commenting on this issue to engage with the facts and the record – the crimes solved, the families reunited, the safeguards already in place, and what our technology actually does,” said MoMo Zhou, the company’s vise president of communications. “We ask them to work with the communities using this technology to decide what rules should govern it, and not to spread misinformation or ban outright a critical public safety tool that is helping to make communities safer.”

Privacy concerns by flock opponents License plate reader tech is decades old, but Flock was created in 2017. But the corporation has been under increasing fire this year – a crescendo of late. Litigation has ensued over constitutional problems about the use of the cameras as awareness has grown about the broad reach of Flock’s network, and incidents of officers exploiting the technology to stalk personal relationships have been chronicled by news media.

Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Texas James Talarico recently said, “Flock cameras have no place in Texas.” Ken Paxton, his Republican opponent, did not reply to a request for comment on the ad.

Pennsylvania Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro, who is up for reelection and seen as a potential White House hopeful in 2028, indicated this week he would support a statewide ban. “Flock cameras are an intrusive invasion of privacy and they should be banned,” said his Republican opponent, Pennsylvania Treasurer Stacy Garrity in a statement.

In Wisconsin, the issue was the centerpiece of this week’s governor’s campaign.

Democratic nominee David Crowley, who’s been criticized for prior comments in support of becoming Wisconsin a worldwide AI and data hub, said he has signed an order prohibiting the use of Flock cameras in Milwaukee County, where he serves as county executive. “As governor I will enact strong transparency and accountability laws that ensure Wisconsinites are not spied on,” he stated.

His Republican opponent, U.S. Rep. Tom Tiffany, said in a statement that he would halt state funding for Flock cameras and create a task force to develop stronger privacy protections, penalties for misuse, limits on data retention and sharing and “safeguards against Communist China and foreign adversaries.”

The Wisconsin Democratic Party responded by sharing a clip of Tiffany from April in which he said he didn’t know what Flock cameras were when asked about them. Then the state Republican Party fired back, saying Big Brother Crowley had a long history of backing police monitoring technology in the past.

The issue also spilled into Rhode Island’s gubernatorial contest, as Democratic Gov. Dan McKee put off installing new cameras until weeks before the state’s Sept. 9 primary, claiming privacy and transparency concerns. His main challenger, ex-CVS executive Helena Foulkes, challenged McKee’s reasons for halting the deployment so close to the election but said a study of how the data is being used was necessary.

William Lawrence, a self-proclaimed democratic socialist and Democratic nominee for the U.S. House in Michigan, recently said the cameras were “the most un-American thing I can imagine.” His opponent, Rep. Tom Barrett, the incumbent, is a co-sponsor of legislation that would prohibit federal agencies from employing Flock cameras or other monitoring equipment, and would bar state and local governments from using federal funding to buy them, a spokeswoman said.

In Florida, where the administration of outgoing Republican Gov. Ron DeSantis this week ordered the removal of electronic license plate readers from state highway rights-of-way, Democratic Senate contender Angie Nixon’s campaign is selling a T-shirt that reads, “FLORIDA NEEDS TO GET THE FLOCK OUT.”

Law enforcement groups back the technology There’s been a growing push for bans or limitations, but other candidates have offered more supportive or nuanced views, and policing groups have reaffirmed their strong support for the company and its tools.

Mitchell Davis III, president of the National Organization of Black Law Enforcement Executives and chief of police in Hazel Crest, Illinois, said in an interview that technology like Flock’s is “invaluable,” but that the talks around it have become “disturbing.”

“I think what’s disturbing for me right now is I’m seeing these communities that are telling their police departments that we’re no longer using these (automated license plate readers) in our communities and I think that’s a huge mistake. He remarked, “That’s like throwing the baby out with the bathwater.” Rather than trying to get rid of the cameras, ensure that there are clear guidelines and proper accountability for misuse of the cameras.

President Donald Trump stated at an Oval Office event this week that his government is looking into the matter and would have more to say soon.

“Americans are living more and more in a world where they are engaging with new technologies like Flock cameras whether they want to or not,” said Cayce Myers, an attorney who teaches courses that touch on public relations, political communications and privacy problems at Virginia Tech University.

“I think there’s a real synergy between the camera systems we’re talking about and also this greater societal discomfort with AI and technology in general,” he added. “It seems obvious that new surveillance technologies will continue to be a major political issue.

“One of the things that concerns him about civil liberties and emerging technologies in his stretch of West Texas, where data center projects are popping up everyplace, is Flock cameras,” Josh Chavez said. Chavez, an HVAC installer, is vociferous enough about data centers that he was invited to participate in a recent roundtable discussion on the topic with Democratic gubernatorial candidate Gina Hinojosa, herself a critic of Flock.

This is the government wanting more authority, isn’t it? Chavez said, the cameras. “Who is looking for more money in partnership with Big Tech, all at our expense.

Why this matters

A local procurement decision can become part of a nationwide surveillance system. Rules written after deployment may be weaker than protections required in the contract.

What to consider next

Watch whether proposed limits include enforceable deletion schedules, public-use reports, warrant standards and penalties for improper searches.
