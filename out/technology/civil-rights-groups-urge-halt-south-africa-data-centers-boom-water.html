Civil rights groups urge a halt to South Africa data centers boom amid water and power fears

Meta’s Stanton Springs Data Center is visible Jan. 13, 2026, in Newton County, Ga.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published September 6, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • South Africa hosts an estimated 70% of Africa's data-center capacity, according to the president.
  • The national human-rights commission received more than 250 submissions about the sector.
  • Critics are asking for binding disclosure of electricity, water and land needs before new approvals.

JOHANNESBURG — Civil society organizations have expressed concern over the lack of openness around the projects as South Africa positions itself as Africa's primary data hub and U.S. technology corporations like Amazon, Microsoft, and Equinix are increasingly establishing digital infrastructure in the nation.

The South African Human Rights Commission, the nation's rights watchdog, told The Associated Press that since it requested feedback in May of this year, it has received more than 250 submissions from interested parties.

70% of Africa's data center capacity is located in South Africa, according to President Cyril Ramaphosa. The government has recognized this as an opportunity in the global digital economy and has invited more tech businesses to establish digital infrastructure in the nation.

But as in other regions of the world, like the United States, where data centers have been expanding quickly, more attention is being paid to their long-term effects on the environment and society.

The commission reported that the creation of data centers, cloud infrastructure, and associated digital technologies had grown significantly throughout the nation.

Dr. Eileen Carter, who oversees the commission's preliminary process, stated that a key issue emerging from the process is precisely the availability, consistency, and transparency of information relating to matters such as electricity and water demand, land use, infrastructure requirements, environmental impacts and impacts on surrounding communities.

An Equinix hyperscale facility to be constructed in Cape Town, a city that survived a so-called Day Zero in 2018 when its taps were expected to run dry due to water constraint, is one of the most recent municipal permits for a data center that has irritated diverse quarters.

Concerns regarding the estimated 160 megawatts of electricity needed to operate the facility have also been raised by the nation's recent experience with widespread power outages, or loadshedding.

With Eskom, the nation's power company, claiming a power surplus during the winter peak-demand season, South Africa appears to have conquered its electrical problem. However, civil society organizations are concerned about the surplus power being sold to data centers.

According to some analysts, the request for a temporary halt to more permits should be interpreted as a way to set clear guidelines, particularly regarding transparency, rather than as a rejection of any foreign investment in the nation.

Clear regulations do not discourage serious investors. According to Pitso Tsibolane, a senior lecturer in information systems at the University of Cape Town, they price uncertainty, and an unregulated boom is the most uncertain environment of all.

According to him, one of the main issues was that operators were not obligated to reveal the amount of water, power, and land they would use or commit to in legally binding terms, thus each request was evaluated without the data.

Due to the significantly greater water, land, and power requirements in South Africa, operators have advised against drawing comparisons between data centers being constructed there and large-scale data centers in the United States.

They point out that most data centers are becoming more and more dependent on renewable energy for electricity and that Eskom now has a power surplus.

The chairperson of the Internet Service Providers Association, Sasha Booth-Beharilal, stated, It should be evident that the growth in energy demand due to data centers is not contributing to electricity scarcity in South Africa and that this increasing demand is not a cause of rising electricity tariffs.

Local data centers use new technologies to reduce their water usage, which is significantly less than the global average.

The group operates data centers across the nation and represents digital companies in the industry.

Why this matters

AI and cloud growth create jobs and infrastructure, but the costs are local. Transparent resource accounting determines whether the benefits are shared or shifted onto residents.

What to consider next

Watch the commission's recommendations and whether municipalities require public reporting of projected and actual water and power use.
