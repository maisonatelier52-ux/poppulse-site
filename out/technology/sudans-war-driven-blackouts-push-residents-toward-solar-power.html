Sudan’s war-driven blackouts push residents toward solar power but high costs shut many out

A technician cleans solar panels installed on the roof of a home in Omdurman, on the outskirts of Khartoum, Sudan, July 23, 2026.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 29, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • The U.N. Development Programme estimates $3 billion in damage to Sudan's electricity grid.
  • Officials told AP generation had fallen from 4,400 MW before the war to about 1,100 MW.
  • A household solar system can cost thousands of dollars in a country with a weakening currency.

OMDURMAN, Sudan — For nearly a year, 31-year-old Sudanese housewife Randa al-Mansour has survived regular blackouts caused by the war-torn country's collapsing electricity grid thanks to solar energy panels on the roof of her building.

“We are suffering big time,” said al-Mansour who lives with her three children in Omdurman, just outside the capital of Khartoum.

“When children come to school in the morning, they discover that there is no electricity there and they have to go back home, where there is none as well. “Such a high temperature makes it very hard for them to study.

The Sudanese military and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have been at war since April 2023, killing at least 59,000 people, though relief groups think the number is likely higher, and displacing some 13 million.

The fighting has also damaged an already inadequate electricity infrastructure, prompting many Sudanese to seek alternative sources of energy, especially during the blistering summer heat. The UN Development Program has put the damage to the energy grid at $3 billion.

‘We had to adjust to this predicament by resorting to solar energy. But not everyone in Sudan can afford this solution, said al-Mansour who supports her family through remittances from her husband working in Saudi Arabia.

Billboards have sprung up across the city in recent months offering solar panels, batteries and inverters as more Sudanese look to switch to solar.

“For a low-income family, the installation of a solar panel system is a huge sacrifice but an inevitable choice,” said Mohey Eddin Abu-Bark, who runs a solar systems business in Khartoum. A typical residential 10-kilowatt solar system costs roughly $5,000.

But for Manahel Madany, a 43-year-old diabetic public servant, the transition is unaffordable despite needing regular energy to keep her medication in her fridge.

“I have to throw away all of my insulin stock every three or four days because it goes bad due to heat and power outages,” Madany said.

She lives in Omdurman in a two-bedroom house with her mother and sister and like many Sudanese, the three are forced to sleep in their house courtyard to escape the searing heat indoors.

“Most of the medicines are not available because not every pharmacy can afford to install solar panels to store medicines,” Madany said.

The Sudan Doctors’ Network, which reports on violence across the country, cautioned that the ongoing electricity shortages could affect patients inside hospitals, particularly kidney patients who need daily care.

The World Bank says that at least 30% of Sudan’s population has no access to electricity, with rural areas the most affected. Conditions have also deteriorated in grid-connected urban centers, with power outages of up to 16 hours in various regions of the country in recent months, two electrical department officials told the Associated Press.

RSF drones have hit many power plants, and electricity production has dropped from 4,400 megawatts before the war to 1,100 megawatts, according to the officials. Almost half of Sudan’s best electricity engineers and technicians have also fled the country since 2023, the officials said. The information came from a survey by a local labor union.

The two talked on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to talk with the media.

The U.N. said Sudan had unlimited potential for solar energy generation, with hours of sunshine typically exceeding 4,000 hours per year. But the hefty upfront costs of imported solar energy systems and a sinking local currency are hindering the move to clean energy.

Why this matters

Energy failure is multiplying the humanitarian effects of war. Electricity is not a separate convenience; it underpins health care, education, water systems and heat resilience.

What to consider next

Humanitarian programs should report how many essential facilities receive reliable power and maintenance support, not only how much solar capacity is installed.
