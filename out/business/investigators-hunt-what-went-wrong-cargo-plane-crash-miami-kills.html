Investigators hunt for what went wrong after cargo plane crash in Miami kills at least 5

Authorities said at least five people were killed after an Amazon cargo plane barreled off a Miami airport runway and collided with cars. The plane overran a runway at Miami International Airport on Sunday. Flights were paused at the busy travel hub.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 16, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • The aircraft traveled about 1,300 feet beyond the paved runway, according to the NTSB chair.
  • Five people on the ground were killed and several others were injured.
  • The cause had not been determined; video observations are leads, not findings.

MIAMI — On Monday, federal investigators are outlining their strategies for figuring out what led an Amazon cargo plane to crash off a runway at Miami International Airport, killing at least five people and wounding five more as it collided with cars and caught fire.

Around 2:00 p.m. on Sunday, the plane, which had just arrived from San Juan, Puerto Rico, overran the runway and crashed into a road that is utilized by the airport's surrounding businesses and warehouse employees. According to officials, there were five fatalities, three serious injuries, and two hospitalized individuals with less serious injuries.

It wasn't immediately apparent whether the pilots who were killed or those who escaped were all in cars when the jet crashed.

According to Miami-Dade Rescue Fire Chief Ray Jadallah, the collision confined the captain and copilot in the cockpit as well as some passengers in cars. The incident caused dense black smoke to billow into the sky. According to the organization, workers also had to free someone who was stuck beneath a car.

Chairwoman Jennifer Homendy will host a press conference on Monday, according to the National Transportation Safety Board.

According to experts, one of the things that investigators will probably look at is whether the aircraft traveled too far before landing. The landing zone is usually found within a runway's first 3,000 feet (914 meters).

According to aviation safety specialist Steve Arroyo, the aircraft ought to have canceled the landing and tried again if it was outside of that range.

According to Mary Schiavo, a pilot and former Inspector General of the Transportation Department, a video that was uploaded online shows the Amazon aircraft hovering above the runway for a considerable amount of time without raising its nose to push the main landing gear to the ground.

Although it wasn't pouring at the moment, there were reports of strong gusts and ominous storm clouds. According to Schiavo, the plane might have floated farther down the runway if it had a tail wind.

If you've already given up a good chunk of the runway by not touching down and landing right away, a tail wind would definitely add to your troubles, Schiavo stated.

Arresting systems, which the FAA claims have been installed at more than 120 airports nationwide to safely stop planes that do fly past the end of a runway, are not present at the Miami airport. At least 497 lives aboard planes that overran runways have been saved thanks to those beds of lightweight, crushable material at the end of a runway, according to the FAA.

For the majority of the afternoon, flights at the popular transport hub were suspended due to the disaster. When about 200 emergency personnel arrived, they discovered that the aircraft had a complex engine compartment fire with high flames and smoke, according to the department. Hours after the fire was put out using foam from specialized airport firefighting units, crews were still dealing with a fuel leak aboard the aircraft.

Images showed that the Prime Air aircraft landed next to a road next to two semitrucks. Despite the fire, the aircraft was mostly intact and seemed to be sitting on its belly.

21 Air, a North Carolina-based cargo carrier, had operated the flight. According to flight-tracking website Flightradar24, the 32-year-old Boeing 767 was once designed to transport passengers and flew for multiple airlines for over 20 years before being converted to a cargo plane in 2015.

According to Schiavo, the NTSB will also take a hard look at this plane's maintenance history, especially since it has previously been owned by foreigners, including a Russian corporation.

FlightAware, a website that monitors airline disruptions, reports that by late Sunday, over 160 flights had been canceled and around 325 had been delayed. Some aircraft were rerouted to Orlando International Airport, which is located more than 200 miles (320 km) north of Miami, according to officials there.

In a statement, Amazon stated it would assist with any inquiry.

The firm stated, We are devastated to learn that five people lost their lives in today's incident at Miami International Airport. We extend our sincere condolences to the families, loved ones, and everyone impacted by this terrible loss.

At Muhammad Ali International Airport in Louisville, a UPS aircraft crashed last year after losing its engine while speeding down the runway. Twelve persons on the ground and all three pilots perished in the incident. There were twenty-three more injuries.

Why this matters

Runway overruns can expose risk to people well beyond an aircraft. The investigation may inform airport design, arresting systems, operating procedures and cargo-aircraft oversight.

What to consider next

The earliest useful evidence will be verified recorder data and the NTSB preliminary report. Avoid assigning blame before those records are published.
