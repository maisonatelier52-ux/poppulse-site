Robin Morgan, poet and feminist whose allies included Jane Fonda and Gloria Steinem, dies at 85

Women’s rights activist Robin Morgan attends a Women’s Liberation conference in New York on May 28, 1970.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published July 18, 2026Updated September 30, 20266 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Morgan's son said she died in New York after recent health problems.
  • She edited Sisterhood Is Powerful, a foundational 1970 anthology of the women's movement.
  • Her work joined poetry, organizing, publishing and institution-building across six decades.

NEW YORK — Robin Morgan, a pleasant child star of the 1940s and ’50s who grew up to be an all-American radical as a poet, writer, editor and second-wave feminism, has died. She was 85 years old.

Morgan, who kept writing new books and poems, died Saturday in New York following a series of health problems in the last few weeks, said her son, Blake Morgan. Her demise happened just days after the death of her close friend and fellow activist Gloria Steinem.

In the 1960s and ’70s Morgan was a living example and catalyst for significant transformations in women’s lives and thinking. Once the ‘Ideal American Girl’ and the beloved ‘Little Robin Morgan,’ she was a rule-breaker and a seeker of justice with an impressive list of accomplishments, as well as a prison record and an F.B.I. file, which she was glad to point out.

Morgan was at the core of some of the defining events of what became known as second-wave feminism, building on the accomplishments of the suffragists half a century before. In 1968 she took part in the inaugural protest of the Miss America pageant, with her fellow activists discarding their bras (soon to be mythologized as burning) into a “Freedom Trash Can.” Her 1970 collection of essays, Sisterhood is Powerful, is considered by many to be one of the main texts of the modern women's movement.

But first and foremost, she saw herself as a poet and an artist, and that fed into her advocacy, her son added.

Blake Morgan said she was someone who could take extremely vast thoughts and distilll them down like a poet into a few words. “Hate generalizes, love specifies,” are some of her quotations.

Morgan is credited with coining the term “herstory” She left us a legacy of words, images and actions. Morgan is credited for coining the phrase “herstory,” the feminist rewriting of “history,” and popularizing the clinched fist/female sign icon of the liberation movement. Her poem “Monster,” with lines such as “I want a women’s revolution like a lover/I lust for it,” was repeated at feminist rallies and beyond. Her poem Arraignment managed to encapsulate the movement's love of the late Sylvia Plath and condemnation of her husband, poet Ted Hughes, whom Morgan and others blamed for Plath's 1963 suicide. Morgan’s rhyming couplet “I accuse/Ted Hughes” inspired the title of Sonic Youth’s “J’Accuse Ted Hughes.”

One government assessment called Morgan a “anarchist housewife.” She fought the establishment, both left and right. She and other demonstrators were detained in 1970 when they occupied the offices of the avant-garde Grove Press, whose owner, Barney Rosset, was battling efforts by employees to unionize. She went after libertines and radicals, from Hugh Hefner to Abbie Hoffman, in the much-reprinted 1970 article “Goodbye to All That,” comparing them to “any other up-and-coming movie star who discards the first wife and kids.”

She had an atypical personal life Morgan was married for over 20 years to the gay poet Kenneth Pitchford, with whom she had her son, a musician, producer and record label founder, and had both male and female lovers in her personal life. She once referred to her marriage as a “two member commune” and was so resistant to labels that she remembers being “straight-baited” by feminists who thought she should be a lesbian and reprimanded by conservatives who assumed she was a lesbian.

In her 2001 book, Saturday’s Child, she wrote of her delight and wonder at how her life ended out and at how she was able to keep “a threadbare, somewhat surreal sense of humor.”

Put that in your apple pie and smoke it, she wrote.

She is the author of more than 20 books including a memoir Besides Saturday's Child, she wrote more than 20 volumes, including The Word of a Woman: Feminist Dispatches and the poetry collections Monster and Dark Matter. Following Sisterhood is Powerful, she published “Sisterhood is Global (1984) and Sisterhood is Forever (2002), with contributions from Steinem, Simone de Beauvoir and Eve Ensler. She was editor-in-chief of Ms. Magazine for some years.

Steinem would label her a perfect “utility player,” blessed in practically every manner a protest movement might use. As an activist, she was a co-founder of 1960s feminist groups such New York Radical Women and Women’s International Terrorist Conspiracy from Hell (W.I.T.C.H.). She was involved with the Youth International Party, the left-wing “Yippies” led by Hoffman and Jerry Rubin among others, but quickly grew dissatisfied with how the party handled women and left.

Founder of an early feminist think tank, the Sisterhood Is Global Institute, in 1984. In 2005, she co-founded the Women’s Media Center with Steinem and Jane Fonda, a non-profit that aims to “support the visibility, viability and decision-making power of women and girls in media.”

More recently she aired a syndicated radio show, and remained busy even after she was diagnosed, 16 years ago, with Parkinson’s disease (which inspired several poems and a TEDWomen talk in 2015).

Hit it big as a child star in the ’40s She may have been just another forgotten child star another time. Born in Lake Worth, Florida in 1941, she was reared in New York and grew up without having seen her father; her mother told her he was dead, although that was a lie. Her mother also shaved a year off her age, saying she was born in 1942, to make her early public career look even more spectacular.

Blonde, brilliant Robin began modeling at age 3, hosted her own radio show, Little Robin Morgan, at age 5 and was featured on the radio (later television) game show Juvenile Jury about the same time. She was seen on the hit TV home comedy series “Mama” from 1949-57 and also appeared on “Robert Montgomery Presents,” “Kraft Theater” and other series.

She met some of the era's top entertainers from Abbott and Costello to Sal Mineo and was popular enough to inspire commercial tie-ins like Robin Morgan toys. Magazines called her “unspoiled,” “courteous,” “the ideal girl-next-door,” immune to “violent outbursts of antagonism against established society.”

But off camera, and occasionally on, she was so divorced from her image that her first “serious” youth poetry began: “Go, rage, winter the world with despair.” On one episode of “Juvenile Jury,” the question was whether a boy deserved a spanking. Morgan was going to quip that he should be wearing padding under his slacks but instead she declared that all physical punishment was wrong.

Producer Dan Ehrenreich, as she recalled in “Saturday’s Child,” grabbed her shoulder so tightly that Morgan could feel his fingernails. He turned off her mike and finally placed his palm over her mouth.

“I loved and respected that child who fought back and that was the first political act I ever dared to make,” wrote Morgan.

She began her political participation in her late teens In her late teens, she took studies at Columbia University, visited poetry workshops and got involved in politics. She marched for civil rights and against the Vietnam War, and met protestors, artists and such poets as W.H. Auden and Anthony Hecht, who gave her a man’s eye perspective of the creative process, “But, my dear, women should be poems, not write them.”

They met at a party in Manhattan in 1962. The other guests, boozy bikers and stoned, hallucinating Joffrey dancers, had gone home, and the host suggested that she go with them. But Pitchford lighted lights and put Dvorak’s New World symphony on the record player and became her First. They strolled for hours, ending up on a park bench downtown.

Robin, you have such a sense of possibilities about you. It makes me feel like anything could happen.

Why this matters

Morgan's career shows how cultural work and political organizing can reinforce each other. She did not simply comment on a movement; she helped build its language, publications and institutions.

What to consider next

Readers approaching her work can start with Sisterhood Is Powerful for the movement archive and Saturday's Child for Morgan's account of her own transformation.
