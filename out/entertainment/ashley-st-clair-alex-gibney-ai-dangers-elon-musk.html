Ashley St. Clair and Alex Gibney on AI and the dangers of Elon Musk

Director Alex Gibney poses for photographers at the photo call for the documentary film ‘Musk’ during the 83rd edition of the Venice Film Festival in Venice, Italy, on Tuesday, Sept. 8, 2026.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 7, 2026Updated September 30, 20265 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Musk premiered at the Venice Film Festival with a runtime of nearly four hours.
  • The documentary uses a digital representation built from Musk's prior public statements.
  • Ashley St. Clair appears in the film and discussed her legal and personal disputes with Musk.

VENICE, Italy — The choice was deliberately ironic, filmmaker Alex Gibney said Tuesday in Venice, before the film's international premiere.

It echoes Musk’s theory that we’re living in a simulation, and pokes fun at his often contradictory and fluid views on the technology. Gibney said it’s to show the “reality of what he’s done and unreality of his narrative.”

Before the filmmakers went too far down the road, they ran the idea by lawyers and got the green light to use the Musk avatar.

The Oscar-winning filmmaker was at the Venice Film Festival with former conservative influencer Ashley St. Clair, with whom Musk shares a child and who also features in the film. She talks about their connection, his character and the nondisclosure agreement she was asked and refused to sign to guaranty paternity support”.

“I was given a chance to not speak for $40 million,” she claimed.

St. Clair sued Musk’s AI startup earlier this year, claiming its Grok chatbot allowed users to create sexually exploitive deepfake photos of her that have caused her embarrassment and emotional suffering.

The film explores Musk’s takeover of X and what Gibney calls manipulation of the algorithm.

“One thing that I didn’t really understand when I started the film was how Musk was manipulating algorithms at Twitter to, in fact, elevate vitriol and hate over, you know, the town square,” Gibney said.

Zoë Schiffer, a “Wired” reporter also in the film, stated that “he has tweaked the algorithm, both to suppress mainstream journalism and then to augment some of the more inflammatory comments and voices on that platform.”

“I think he’s stoking fires and causing deliberate division that could be catastrophic if it’s not regulated or contained,” St. Clair added.

Clocking in at than four hours, “Musk” examines his horrific childhood, including an interview with his father, Errol Musk, and follows his early days in Silicon Valley through his DOGE work with the second Donald Trump administration and involvement in right-wing politics around the world. It was intended to be a lot shorter film until Musk immersed himself in Trump’s campaign, Gibney added.

Among those interviewed are Tesla co-founder Martin Eberhard, his first wife Justine Musk, neurologist Sam Harris, tech columnist Kara Swisher and former USAID Administrator Samantha Power.

“I think he is extremely dangerous for democracy,” Gibney stated. The movie raises the alarm with the information the “DOGE boys” obtained on U.S. residents with their unrestrained access to so many federal agencies. The film will be released in theaters in October.

Why this matters

The film is both about concentrated technology power and an example of synthetic media used in nonfiction. Its method will influence how audiences assess consent, quotation and authenticity in future documentaries.

What to consider next

When the film reaches theaters, look for a published sourcing note and clear labeling of reconstructed material. Viewers should be able to distinguish archival record, simulation and filmmaker interpretation.
