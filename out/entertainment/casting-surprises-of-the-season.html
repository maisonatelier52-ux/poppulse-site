Amid backlash, museum cancels planned Met Gala exhibit on fashion designer John Galliano

Fashion designer John Galliano poses at the end of the presentation of the Dior Haute Couture spring/summer 2010 fashion collection on Jan. 25, 2010, in Paris.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published August 12, 2026Updated September 30, 20265 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • The Metropolitan Museum of Art and John Galliano said they mutually agreed not to proceed with the exhibition.
  • Critics argued that a gala-launched solo show would amount to celebration, not simply historical examination.
  • The dispute centered on how institutions distinguish documenting influential work from conferring prestige.

NEW YORK - The Metropolitan Museum of Art is scrapping plans for an exhibition honoring British designer John Galliano, plans that had drawn sharp opposition from the Jewish community and others over the artist’s history of antisemitic and racist remarks.

The museum confirmed Monday that the high-profile spring Costume Institute exhibit, “John Galliano: Horizons,” to be unveiled by the annual Met Gala next May, will be replaced with another exhibit.

“After thoughtful consideration with John Galliano, we have mutually decided not to move forward with the exhibition,” said Max Hollein, director and CEO of the museum, in a statement.

The designer added: “After much reflection and discussion with The Met and everyone involved, I have decided with great sadness that it is best that the exhibition not take place at this time. “I remain fully accountable for the pain I caused with my words in the past, particularly the hurt I caused to the Jewish and Asian communities, and I remain deeply sorry for it.”

Galliano found guilty in French anti-Semitism trial Galliano has long been considered one of the fashion world’s greatest talents for his flamboyant creative ideas and spent years at the top of that world as creative director of Christian Dior. He was sacked from the role after a series of racist and antisemitic comments he made in 2010 and 2011 emerged and went viral, leading to his conviction in an antisemitism trial in France.

In the footage of the comments Galliano made to bar patrons were the words “I love Hitler.” He warned diners, who were Jewish, that their mothers and forefathers would have been “gassed.” He also told one Asian customer to get out of his place.

Galliano, who was dropped by Dior, spent several years in private and public efforts at rehabilitation. In 2014 he bounced back, being recruited by Paris fashion label Maison Margiela, where he remained for 10 years.

He was to be only the third living designer to have a solo show at the Costume Institute.‘Brave,’ says Anna Wintour of Galliano’s choice to resign The star-studded Met Gala opens the spring exhibit, the most visible event on the fashion calendar, as famous visitors stroll the show’s galleries during cocktail hour each year. The gala is coordinated by Anna Wintour, the Vogue global editorial director and one of the most powerful people in fashion. Wintour has long been a Galliano champion.

In her own statement posted by the museum, she termed Galliano’s choice to stand down a brave one.

“John’s decision that the exhibition of his work should not happen now is extremely courageous and a measure of the man he is,” Wintour remarked. “I think this is the right decision and he and the Museum have my full support,” he said.

Andrew Bolton, the veteran curator of the Costume Institute, also stated he agreed with the decision in light of “the pain and concern this exhibition has caused.”

When the Met announced the display in late July, it was careful to note that the exhibit will address Galliano’s prior faults head-on. It also consulted with Jewish community leaders before publicizing the program in a failed effort to head off anger.

One of the main detractors of the planned show was New York City Council Speaker Julie Menin, who issued a statement Monday saying she had met several times with the museum to voice opposition to an exhibit commemorating Galliano.

“This was a gut punch to the Jewish community at this rising time of antisemitism and from the beginning, I’ve told them that I strongly disagreed with this decision,” said Menin, who highlighted she was the daughter of a holocaust survivor. “This is the right decision and the pain and harm this was causing was just unacceptable.”

Complicating the museum’s situation, the gala is traditionally held on the first Monday in May, which in 2027 falls on Holocaust Remembrance Day. There has been talk of the museum changing the date for that reason.

Galliano show critics respond Jonathan Greenblatt, chief executive of the Anti-Defamation League, said Monday in a post on X that the planned display had been “the wrong move at the wrong time.”

“Antisemitism is at crisis levels and for the Met to honor John Galliano at this moment was misguided, and even Galliano himself ultimately recognized what the Met should have done from the start,” Greenblatt added. He met with Hollein and a Met board member a few weeks ago and told Hollein to cancel, according to a person familiar with the incident.

In prior comments upon the show's announcement, Greenblatt had lauded Galliano's attempts to repair the harm from his words, claiming the ADL had long since accepted the designer's apologies. But he also acknowledged the moment was challenging for such an accolade and asked the museum to respect that fact.

Another notable vocal critic of the event was David Ingber, a rabbi and senior director for Jewish life at the Bronfman Center at 92NY, the major New York Jewish organization. Galliano’s decision to pull out of the award was hailed by Ingber who said: “It’s good to see he knows the cost to other people.”

Of the Met, he said the problem of redemption was very different from presenting a rare and extremely prominent distinction. “Redemption doesn’t give somebody the right to all the honors that we have,” he remarked.

“It wasn’t just the Met looking at Galliano’s work,” said Ingber, who was not involved in the museum’s outreach to leaders in the Jewish community. It was a solo exhibition to be inaugurated at the gala and it would be an act of institutional celebration of his work. Galliano hasn’t been wiped from the face of the earth … the debate was whether the Met should design its glitziest annual gala celebration around him.”

Why this matters

Cultural institutions do more than preserve history: their calendars, galleries and galas confer legitimacy. The decision will shape how museums explain the difference between critical study and institutional endorsement.

What to consider next

The Met has not yet announced the replacement exhibition. The central editorial question will be how it documents the controversy and whether future retrospectives use clearer curatorial framing from the outset.
