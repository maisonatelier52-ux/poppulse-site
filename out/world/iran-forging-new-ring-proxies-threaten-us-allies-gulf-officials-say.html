Iran is forging a new ring of proxies to threaten US allies in the Gulf, officials say

Houthi supporters attend a rally against the Saudi-led coalition in Sanaa, Yemen, July 31, 2026.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published May 29, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Regional officials told AP that Houthi personnel helped Iraqi militias coordinate drone attacks on Saudi targets.
  • Representatives of the Popular Mobilization Forces denied involvement.
  • The reported shift follows heavy losses among other Iran-aligned groups after 2023.

CAIRO — Now the Houthis have helped the Iraqi militias organize and execute the two-day swarm attack, regional officials tell The Associated Press, a sign of a new level of collaboration.

Iran had been arming militant groups on Israel’s borders for decades, groups that sustained heavy casualties in the hostilities that ensued after Hamas’ onslaught from Gaza on Oct. 7, 2023. Israel pummeled the Palestinian terrorists as well as Lebanon’s Hezbollah, Iran’s most powerful ally.

“Now, Iran is using the Iraqi militias and the Houthis to threaten Saudi Arabia and other American allies in the Gulf in an effort to drive up the costs of the war launched by the U.S. and Israel in February,” say regional officials and experts.

That is a hazardous way to do things. Houthi attacks on Saudi oil facilities on Tuesday threatened to spark full-scale war with the country. Iraq has ordered militias to disarm by the end of this month, but major Iran-backed groups have refused to do so. New U.S. move to economically isolate Iran risks greater escalation

Iran-backed militants in Yemen and Iraq begin pincer movement Two Saudi sources citing intelligence and a top Iraqi security official acknowledged the Houthi role in the Iraqi drone attack. They alleged that Houthi emissaries worked out of an operations center controlled by Iraqi militants.

Saudi Arabia and the US replied with combined airstrikes that killed at least 20 Iraqi forces, six Iranian advisers and at least one Houthi official. A Houthi official and an Iraqi militia member verified the death of the Houthi, which was not previously reported.

Previous strikes in Iraq had killed U.S. military officials say Houthis. The individual said the U.S. is concerned about the Houthi presence there and their capacity to strike other nations, especially Saudi Arabia.

Two officials with the Popular Mobilization Forces, an umbrella group of militias that is formally part of Iraq’s security forces, rejected any role in the attack on Saudi Arabia and disputed that the Houthis were acting under its auspices. The PMF contains powerful Iran-backed militias that occasionally operate independently.

All the officials and the militia member talked on condition of anonymity as they were not authorized to speak to media. The Houthis did not immediately react to a request for comment.

During the battle in Gaza, cooperation increased According to Houthi and regional sources, the cooperation between the Iraqi militias and the better-organized Houthis had strengthened during the conflict in Gaza, when they coordinated strikes on Israel. At the height of the war, Houthi leader Abdel-Malek al-Houthi talked about a combined operations room.

The U.S. and Israel attacked Iran on Feb. 28 to widen the battle and inflict damage on the main oil producers that had American military on their soil. Iran and its allies then began fire on the Gulf nations.

Iran has a hybrid approach in this fight. “We are not fighting on one front and we are not using one tool,” Mahdi Mohammadi, an assistant to Iran’s parliament speaker, posted on social media in June.

“The Gaza war gave birth to the Houthis and opened a new horizon in Iraq. “Many groups there became eager to support the Houthis and in turn the Houthis started sharing their military expertise with Iraqi militias,” said Ahmed Nagi, a senior Yemen analyst at the Crisis Group, an international research tank.

Now they are “squeezing Saudi like with pliers,” Nagi added.

Houthi siege of Saudi Arabia ratchets up pressure Just a week before the drone swarm strike, the Houthis announced a blockade of Saudi shipping in the Red Sea, hitting another key economic artery after the military disruption of the Strait of Hormuz had sent shockwaves through the world economy.

The war watchdog group ACLED said the Houthis have launched more than a dozen attacks on Saudi oil installations and vessels in the Red Sea since late July.

Saudi authorities announced on Tuesday that they had launched a wave of strikes on oil and other installations in southern Saudi Arabia that wounded more than 70 people, including women and children.

Due to the Houthi embargo, ACLED said, Saudi Arabia has had to implement a “dark” transit strategy, shutting off the tracking signals of oil tankers transiting the Red Sea.

Saudi oil exports in the spotlight Much of the Saudi oil had been rerouted to the Red Sea after Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. When the Houthis launched their attacks, the monarchy increased the northward flow of oil to the Suez Canal and Egypt’s SUMED pipeline, which has seen flows soar from 650,000 barrels a day in June to more than 1.9 million in August, according to global shipping monitor Kpler.

Last month, the Houthis demonstrated they can strike that route too, with a missile attack on a Saudi tanker in the northern Red Sea. The ship had apparently sailed from the Saudi port of Yanbu to the Suez Canal, about 1,000 kilometers (700 miles) from Houthi territory.

They are willing to take some near term pain for what they feel will be longer term gains,” such as extending their reach along the Red Sea coast, said Adam Baron, a Yemen expert at the New America think tank in Washington.

“The train to a return to full scale war has left the station, and it’s not clear that anyone is going to jump in to stop it,” he said, pointing to the U.S. focus on Iran and the Saudis' unwillingness to intervene in Yemen again.

The Houthis have their own motivations to fight against Saudi Arabia The rebels took over Yemen’s capital Sanaa in 2014. The Saudis joined Yemen’s internationally recognized government in a counteroffensive the next year, fearing the country may wind up with a Hezbollah-like force at its doorstep.

The civil conflict has killed at least 150,000 people, the U.N. estimates, and at times brought Yemen to the verge of famine. A truce that had largely held till 2022 is now in shreds.

The Saudi-led siege has been pressuring the Houthis for years and taken a tremendous toll on ordinary people in rebel-held portions of Yemen.

The Houthis generally retaliate with more violence and further conflict might allow them to take control of energy-rich parts of eastern Yemen, said Elisabeth Kendall, a Yemen researcher at Cambridge University’s Girton College.

The Houthis have large quantities of sophisticated weapons For years, the Houthis, who hold northern and central Yemen, have been smuggling in modern weapons in breach of the blockade and a UN arms embargo. Iran denies arming the rebels, although Iranian-made weapons have been detected on the battlefield and in intercepted shipments.

The rebels have several cruise and ballistic missiles, unmanned submarines and drones.

Saudi-backed forces in Yemen are fighting back on a number of fronts including the key port city of Hodeida and the province of Taiz along Yemen’s west coast. Houthis hit Mokha, the largest Red Sea port still held by government forces.

Saudi Arabia seems unwilling to hit the Houthis directly again, but if the rebels continue to escalate their activities, “it will have to act decisively once and for all,” Kendall warned.

Why this matters

Coordination across national borders can change both the reach of armed groups and the likelihood that a local strike triggers regional retaliation.
