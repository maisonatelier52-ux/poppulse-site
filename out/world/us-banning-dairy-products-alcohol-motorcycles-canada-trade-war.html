US banning dairy products, most alcohol and motorcycles from Canada in growing trade war

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney says U.S. demands could hollow out key Canadian industries and limit the country’s independence, while Trump’s threats to Canadian sovereignty have rallied the public behind him.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 1, 2026Updated September 30, 20265 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • The U.S. bans cover most Canadian alcohol, some dairy products, motorcycles and other goods.
  • The measures follow Canadian tariffs on about $20 billion in U.S. imports.
  • Both governments are also redirecting procurement and trade relationships.

TORONTO — The White House announced Tuesday the United States is barring imports of some dairy goods and motorbikes from Canada and most alcoholic drinks in a trade dispute that shows little indication of abating.

The ban takes effect Sept. 29 and follows retaliatory tariffs pledged by Canada that went into force earlier Tuesday on $20 billion in U.S. imports. Canada responded to tariff steps by U.S. President Donald Trump after trade discussions between the two countries fell apart last month.

Trump also sought to prohibit Canadian items from significant, long-term U.S. government contracts, ordering the U.S. General Services Administration to deem Canadian products ineligible for those contracts unless Canada provides “full and fair reciprocity″ for American products.

Mark Carney, the prime minister of Canada, said Canada’s policy was about becoming more independent and promised to speed up those efforts. It’s to make sure no country can take us hostage. And we can live the way we wish to live,” he added.

The rift has jolted one of the world’s closest relationships. The U.S. placed 50% tariffs on around 5% of Canadian imports on Aug. 22, citing Canada’s unfair treatment of American dairy, alcoholic beverage and auto industries.

The U.S. and Canada have long clashed over trade, particularly Canada’s protected dairy market and subsidies to softwood lumber producers. They remained friends and faithful allies, however.

The U.S.-Canada relationship has taken a nosedive under Trump. Trump has also made repeated incendiary remarks about declaring Canada the 51st U.S. state, as well as slapping tariffs on Canadian exports. Carney won a come-from-behind political win last year promising to stand up to him.

Some Canadian provinces restricted the sale of U.S. alcohol products — and the U.S. responded Tuesday by banning many Canadian wines and spirits. The restriction also extends to several motorcycles and mopeds, dairy products including whey and various types of molasses.

Canada is considering ways to forge a relationship with the European Union that would fall short of full membership, a Canadian official familiar with the talks said.

This can entail extending current agreements, agreeing a new treaty or setting up alternative types of collaboration. The official said Canada is already in discussions with provinces, territories and labor groups about what a greater partnership with the EU may look like, but no model has been selected.

The official, who was not allowed to speak publicly on the talks, spoke on condition of anonymity.

Carney is set to go to Strasbourg, France, next week to hear European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s State of the European Union address on Sept. 16 and address the European Parliament the following day.

Canada turns away from US Carney said the trade sanctions will bring short-term pain but added they would drive Canada to go quicker on investment, infrastructure and trade diversification.

“It was easy business, but it meant we were too dependent on one economic partner,” he remarked. “Those days are gone.”

More than 70% of Canadian exports still head to the United States, highlighting the magnitude of Carney’s push to diversify trade. Canada’s exports to foreign countries are growing rapidly and are poised to increase over the next decade, he added.

Trump strikes back, Canada hits back Carney said Canada could not let U.S. goods come in tariff-free while Canadian companies suffer tariffs in the U.S.

Canada was not trying to ratchet up the stand-off, he added, but the duties were needed to defend Canadian jobs.

The bigger problem, Carney added, was what Washington had wanted in the failed negotiations.

“But the most fundamental problem was that the cumulative U.S. demands showed they wanted us to be more dependent on them, not less. “In too many areas they wanted dependency, not a real economic partnership.”

The prime minister claimed Washington was trying to curtail French language and cultural rights, control over future trade treaties and measures that would damage the car, steel and forestry sectors.

The resolution of the trade war might have ramifications well beyond Canada, testing whether a smaller U.S. partner can withstand Trump’s economic pressure without having to give in.

Earlier Tuesday, a Canadian official said Ottawa planned to stay the course whether Trump replied with nothing or what the official called a “nuclear response.” “The government’s policy will be to focus more on domestic production and expansion of international trade,” the official said.

The tariffs targeted hundreds of American products, from steel, aluminum and cheese to appliances, apparel, cosmetics and farm equipment, at rates of 15 percent, 25 percent or 50 percent. They represent some $20 billion worth of American goods, or approximately 6% of the $333.6 billion the United States shipped to Canada last year.

Since trade talks between Canada and the U.S. ended Aug. 21, Trump and his administration have levied more tariffs and launched a barrage of threats and assaults that have painted Canada as weak and reliant.

The outrage has been building across the country at Trump’s trade war and his frequent mention of making Canada the 51st state. Carney lauded the steep drop in travel to the United States by Canadians and the boycott of U.S. goods as marks of national resolve.

The new signs the province will put up at U.S. border crossings will read: “Welcome to British Columbia, Canada,” B.C. Premier David Eby stated. Strong, proud, and will NEVER be the 51st state. Sorry!

“We’re kind people, but you never, ever mistake kindness for weakness, and that’s one of our greatest strengths,” Eby added.

And neither side is rushing back to the table Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc said the government was reviewing the latest U.S. tariff measures and that he was in communication with U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer. Canada stands ready to engage when Washington is, LeBlanc said.

U.S. and Canadian trade representatives have had “constructive conversations” and will talk again in coming days to see if there’s a “path forward,” a senior Trump administration official told reporters on a conference call arranged by the White House late Tuesday.

“It’s hard to see much incentive for him to restart talks when his approval rating is above 70%,” said former U.S. trade official Wendy Cutler.

At this stage there is no question that neither side wants to look too keen to re-engage for fear of looking weak, she said.

Why this matters

The U.S. and Canada built deeply integrated supply chains under decades of predictable trade rules. Rapid restrictions impose costs before companies can easily find substitute suppliers or customers.

What to consider next

Watch for the final covered-product schedule, customs guidance, legal challenges and any exemptions. Canada is also exploring closer economic ties beyond the United States.
