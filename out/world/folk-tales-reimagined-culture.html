Iran claims a strike on a US ship in the Strait of Hormuz but the US denies it

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Iran claimed it hit an unmanned U.S. vessel attempting to enter the Strait of Hormuz.
  • The U.S. military rejected the claim and described it as false.
  • No independently verified evidence had resolved the conflicting accounts at publication.

CAIRO — Iran claimed Sunday that it hit an unmanned U.S. vessel attempting to enter the Strait of Hormuz, a charge the U.S. Navy called a “total lie.”

The alleged attack came a day after the U.S. military said it had destroyed three Iranian oil tankers after Navy vessels were targeted with ballistic missiles. It was a frightening escalation, experts said, given Iran’s earlier attacks at sea targeted commercial shipping.

The conflict started with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran on Feb. 28. But intermittent violence has gone on since a weak ceasefire deal was announced in June, with both sides aiming to inflict military and economic hardship as discussions have faltered.

About a week after fighting resumed, strikes were called.After a month of relative quiet, the U.S. and Iran returned this week to attacking one another, again targeting the Strait of Hormuz and regions along Iran’s southern coast. Earlier in the week at least five people were killed in a U.S. bombing that drew renewed attention to an area where a school was hit in the war's opening attacks.

The Trump administration seems to be taking a two-pronged strategy to the dispute, military responding to attacks on the strait while going after foreign financial institutions that do business with Iran’s money

But Iran’s new hard-line senior leaders have demonstrated a determination to dig in after decades of sanctions. Over the years, Iran has discovered ways to get under sanctions and now a U.S. blockade of its ports to get its oil to buyers to help ease mounting economic strains. That in part depends on a shadow navy smuggling its oil.

US: million barrels of oil a day flow across strait Tehran's new leverage is focused on the Strait of Hormuz, which is vital for global oil and natural gas supplies. It was thought to as an international waterway before the conflict.

U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said Sunday that on average some 9 million barrels of oil a day are passing across the strait, which could help to ease pressure on energy prices. TankerTrackers.com and other sources put his estimated flows at higher than usual over the past 28 days via the strait.

“We’re probably two-thirds or more of pre-conflict flows,” Wright told CNN, with oil also flowing through pipelines in the region.

But those flows require the presence of the U.S. Navy so that they can help escort tankers and give some protection against a potential Iranian attack. “I think other countries will come along eventually to support the Navy’s efforts,” Wright added.

Meanwhile, Iran’s nuclear program, the issue that helped spark the war, was slated to be discussed in negotiations that quickly broke apart after the U.S. and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding in mid-June.

Diplomats claim the United States, Britain, France and Germany now want to report Iran to the U.N. Security Council for failing to meet its nuclear nonproliferation responsibilities.

Why this matters

Fast-moving conflict claims can influence markets and military decisions before verification catches up. Precise language reduces the chance that propaganda is repeated as fact.

What to consider next

Look for corroborating evidence from maritime trackers, satellite imagery, shipping companies and independent investigators—not simply repeated official statements.
