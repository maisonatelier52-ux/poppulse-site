Naomi Osaka exits US Open, but not before displaying one more swaggy fashion ensemble

Naomi Osaka, of Japan, arrives for a match against Elena Rybakina, of Kazakhstan, during the fourth round of the U.S. Open tennis championships, Monday, Sept. 7, 2026, in New York.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published June 20, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Osaka wore a multicolored KidSuper cloak before her fourth-round loss to Elena Rybakina.
  • Her tournament wardrobe used basketball references and work from several New York designers.
  • The clothing functioned as both sponsor collaboration and a pre-match ritual Osaka said helped create excitement.

NEW YORK — Naomi Osaka may not have exited the U.S. Open with a bang, a lot of it. But she sure did go out with a bang -- of color, and a little of swag.

The two-time U.S. Open winner lost a lopsided fourth-round match Monday against Elena Rybakina. But she kept making her imprint as the unquestioned fashion queen on tour. Osaka, already known for her walk-on fashion reveals at tournaments, has been boosting her game at the year’s final major with a succession of theatrical ensembles, all with a basketball theme as her sponsor, Nike, recognizes the New York Knicks’ championship win.

For her final look, she arrived on court Monday in a coat of many colors: a fur-trimmed cloak designed by Brooklyn-based company KidSuper.

Osaka said after the match: “I felt really swaggy when I put it on.” “It was a good likeness of New York,” he said.

The past week or so, Osaka has been working with celebrity stylist Law Roach, aka Zendaya’s stylist, for her increasingly theatrical outfits, highlighting the work of numerous designers.

Monday’s eccentric walk-on ensemble from KidSuper, the label of designer Colm Dillane, contained not only the extravagant coat, but a headband that resembled the blue, red and white NBA insignia — only now, with a racket-wielding tennis player, not a basketball player.

For her first-round match a week ago, Osaka resembled NBA star Allen Iverson with a long, white tulle skirt and a dramatic gray, hooded robe. Osaka pulled the hood down, her hair in cornrows under a headband, a look that was a nod to Iverson when he was the NBA MVP for the Philadelphia 76ers. Her outfit was created by Who Decides War, the New York City-based streetwear label.

For round two, Osaka appeared in a Thom Browne costume that featured a flowing white jacket embroidered with crossed tennis rackets. There was a basketball net under the mesh fabric of her tennis skirt.

In the third round, Osaka, a four-time Grand Slam champion, donned the label Monse — an oversized mix of a gray suit jacket and hoodie sweatshirt over flowing gray fleece leggings, an outfit designed to blend business dress with sports wear.

After her loss on Monday, Osaka was asked whether she was sad because there might have been more fashion options she’d been preparing to show off had she gone farther.

“I think there's always something, you know,” Osaka added. “I think that’s a way I can have fun and kind of get myself excited too.”

But the 28-year-old, who admitted she had been handicapped by leg problems during Monday’s match, was philosophical about her loss.

“Maybe it’s just that I am getting older but I don’t feel sad,” Osaka added. “I think also if I look back on my career and the things that I’ve been able to do, there’s nothing that I regret or there’s nothing that I’m sad about ... If I win another Grand Slam, it’s not going to change my life.”

Why this matters

Athlete style is part of the modern sports economy, but it can also be self-expression rather than a substitute for performance. The most useful coverage makes room for both.

What to consider next

Future tournament looks will be judged against this unusually coherent series. The harder standard is whether coverage can discuss design without reducing Osaka's tennis to a caption.
