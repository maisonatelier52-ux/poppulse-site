Carbon credit financing emerges as Africa’s biggest bet for expanding clean cooking

Mary Kavutha prepares breakfast using a fuel-efficient cookstove made by BURN in Kiambu, Kenya, on July 31, 2026.

Prepared by PopPulse Editorial · Editorial desk
Published July 15, 2026Updated September 30, 20264 min read

At a glance

What you need to know

  • Nearly 1 billion Africans still cook with charcoal or firewood, according to the IEA.
  • Carbon-credit revenue can reduce the upfront price of efficient stoves and induction cookers.
  • Projects still face verification concerns and a financing gap before credits are generated.

NAIROBI, Kenya — Each morning, at her house in Nairobi, Mary Kavutha serves breakfast for her two small children on an induction cooker. Two years ago, the businesswoman used a charcoal stove that smoked up her kitchen and cost her around $1.15 a day in fuel.

Today, she claims, she can prepare numerous meals for 80 cents in electricity tokens.

“It cooks a lot faster, and it’s a lot safer because I have young children,” Kavutha added. Charcoal made smoke in the house all the time. Now I can cook inside comfortably and I spend a lot less.”

Almost 1 billion Africans still depend on charcoal or firewood for daily cooking. Household air pollution from these fuels is responsible for almost 850,000 fatalities each year on the continent, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA).

“We switched not for climate reasons, but because the stoves are affordable, subsidized by carbon credit financing that allows clean cooking companies to raise capital against future revenues from emission reductions, like millions of other Africans,” Kavutha said.

Carbon finance, long attacked over credibility concerns around some projects, could become Africa’s most crucial tool for increasing cleaner household energy and relieving strain on forests, say clean-cooking businesses.

Africa is adopting clean cookstoves more rapidly In June, the IEA stated that Africa had obtained US$900 million in fresh financing commitments for clean cooking technologies.

The energy organization noted that more than 30 governments in nations representing roughly 80% of Africans currently lacking access to clean cooking had put in place 121 new clean cooking laws since the Paris climate summit in 2015.

Clean cooking is not a luxury, but an everyday requirement affecting every household, Tanzanian President Samia Suluhu remarked after co-hosting an IEA conference on clean cooking in Dar es Salaam earlier this year. That came after 30 states last year adopted the African Union’s Dar es Salaam Declaration on Clean Cooking.

Clean cooking is getting cheaper because of carbon funding “The change in carbon finance has turned what was once an expensive clean-energy product into an affordable one for millions of low-income households,” said Peter Scott, founder and CEO of BURN, which produces eco-friendly cookstoves.

“Carbon project finance is the only way clean cooking is going to scale on the continent,” Scott added. “Governments make money, and customers get a product they never would have been able to buy without that subsidy.”

The Nairobi-based firm says it has given over 7.3 million cookstoves across 11 African countries.

“The biggest innovation we have is not the stove, it’s the financing around it,” Scott added. “Without that funding, many families can’t afford to switch.”

Carbon subsidies allow buyers to buy an efficient biomass stove that normally sells for about $40 for as little as $5. Costlier induction cookers are paid for through carbon credits and short-term pay-as-you-go agreements that spread payments over several months.

“Investors put in upfront capital that subsidizes the retail cost of our stoves. “In return, they earn revenue from carbon credits generated as households cut back on charcoal, wood and other polluting fuels,” he said.

Credit markets still under the microscope The industry has embraced tougher regulations and more comprehensive measures of emissions, using Bluetooth monitoring, digital verification and real-time usage data to counter accusations of carbon credits.

But critics of the idea argue it is still hazardous to rely too much on carbon credits to finance clean cooking.

One blow was the February shutdown of Kenyan clean cooking company Koko Networks, seen as a poster child for Africa’s green shift, after it lost a battle for a government letter of authority to sell carbon credits.

“Carbon money can be a part of this, but it should not be at the heart of the transition,” said WRI Kenya representative and head of Air Quality for WRI Africa, George Mwaniki.

The difficulty, he explained, is that carbon finance tends to follow the money needed to bring cleaner cooking equipment into households.

Carbon financing is more of a secondary source of financing Mwaniki remarked. “This is a problem for clean-cooking companies that need money to build and distribute equipment before they can create the emissions reductions that ultimately generate carbon credits.”

“If we depend entirely on carbon credits to facilitate the transition then it will be very slow and it won’t happen at the speed that we need it to,” he said.

“I buy less, my children are safer and my kitchen is cleaner,” Kavutha remarked. That’s all that matters.

Clean cooking is on the rise, flaws and all. Companies are tailoring their strategy to local situations. BURN, for instance, claims electric cooking has a more feasible market in Kenya and Tanzania but biomass stoves are more popular in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Madagascar.

Eco Safi, which operates in Uganda, Kenya and Malawi, provides forced-draft pellet stoves and clean, sustainable fuel from agricultural waste. In Rwanda, BioMassters makes locally-made solar-powered pellet burners and a biomass-residue fuel that is smokeless. ENEDOM makes agricultural-residue briquettes in Kigali.

What matters to households like Kavutha’s because a cleaner stove now costs less to own and operate than the charcoal she originally relied on.

Why this matters

The model connects household health, climate finance and energy access. If the accounting is credible, one payment stream can support all three; if it is weak, families and investors carry the risk.

What to consider next

Watch for independent verification, clear government authorization and evidence that subsidies reach households rather than only improving project margins.
